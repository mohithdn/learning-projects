Concept 1

Vectors as meaning

Before anything can be "compared," it has to be turned into numbers.

Plain-language explanation. A vector here is just a list of six numbers, one per genre — Action, Comedy, Romance, Thriller, Drama, Horror — each scored 0 to 5 for how strongly that film fits it. RRR becomes [5, 1, 1, 3, 3, 0]. That's it: a film has become a point in a 6-dimensional space.

Real-life analogy. Think of a restaurant rating you'd give on five separate scales — spice, price, speed, portion size, ambience — instead of one star rating. Two restaurants with similar numbers across all five scales "feel" similar even if you've never described them in words. Genre vectors do the same job for films.

Why it matters. Once meaning is a list of numbers, it can be measured, sorted, and searched with plain arithmetic — which is exactly what lets a computer "recommend" without understanding a single word of plot or dialogue.

Concept 2

Cosine similarity

Measuring the angle between two vectors, ignoring how long they are.

Plain-language explanation. Cosine similarity asks: do these two vectors point in the same direction? It's computed as the dot product divided by the product of the two vectors' lengths — which cancels out length entirely and leaves only the angle.

Real-life analogy. Picture two people describing a movie's genre balance as a pie chart. One says "70% action, 30% drama," the other says "the same 70/30 split, but I only rated the top three genres out of five." Their proportions agree even though their raw numbers don't — cosine similarity is built to spot that agreement.

Why it matters. A short, punchy plot summary and a long, detailed one can still describe the "same kind" of film. Cosine similarity lets a system say they're alike without being thrown off by one being a bigger vector than the other.

Worked code

def cosine_similarity(u, v):
    # cos(theta) = dot(u, v) / (norm(u) * norm(v))
    dot = np.dot(u, v)
    norm_u = np.linalg.norm(u)
    norm_v = np.linalg.norm(v)
    if norm_u == 0 or norm_v == 0:
        return 0.0
    return dot / (norm_u * norm_v)
Pitfall Cosine similarity ranges from -1 to 1, but genre scores here are never negative, so in this app it only ever lands between 0 and 1. Don't assume that range holds for every embedding — real ones can and do produce negative similarities.

Concept 3

Dot product

The same handshake as cosine similarity, minus the part that cancels out length.

Plain-language explanation. The dot product multiplies each matching pair of numbers and adds up the results. Unlike cosine similarity, it never divides by length — so a vector with big numbers across the board will naturally score higher, even against the same direction.

Real-life analogy. Imagine rating "enthusiasm" instead of "proportion." A reviewer who rates everything 5/5 across the board will produce a bigger dot product with anyone else's high ratings than a reviewer who rates everything 1/5, even if both reviewers agree on which genres matter most.

Why it matters. Some recommendation systems want that magnitude sensitivity — a film that's a strong 5/5 action epic should arguably outrank a mild 2/5 action film when someone wants "more action," and dot product is the tool that keeps magnitude in the picture.

Pro tip The app ranks by cosine similarity, not dot product — a deliberate choice, so that a film rated conservatively across genres isn't penalized against one rated enthusiastically. If you ever see odd rankings in a similarity system, check which of these two metrics is actually driving the sort.

Concept 4

Euclidean distance

Forget angles — just measure the straight line between two points.

Plain-language explanation. If you plot two films as points in genre-space, Euclidean distance is the length of the straight line connecting them — the same Pythagorean idea you'd use to measure distance on a map, just extended to six dimensions instead of two. Lower distance means the films sit closer together.

Real-life analogy. Two houses can be "close" by straight-line distance even if no road connects them directly. Euclidean distance in genre-space works the same way — it doesn't care about direction, only how far apart two points actually sit.

Why it matters. Distance-based search is the backbone of nearest-neighbor lookups in many real systems — it's often faster to compute at scale than cosine similarity, which is why it's worth knowing both.

Concept 5

Ranking the whole catalog

This is the actual algorithm behind the "Find Similar Films" button.

Plain-language explanation. Pick one film as the "query." Loop over every other film in the catalog, compute its cosine similarity to the query, and keep a running leaderboard. Once every film has been checked, sort the leaderboard from most to least similar and keep the top few.

Real-life analogy. It's like asking a friend "which of these 39 other films is most like the one I just watched?" and having them hold each one up for comparison, one at a time, before finally announcing their ranked shortlist.

Why it matters. This "compare against everything, then sort" pattern is the essence of nearest-neighbor search — the exact same shape of algorithm search engines and recommendation systems run, just against millions of vectors instead of 39.

Concept 6

This is a toy embedding

Zoom out from six hand-picked numbers to what a real model produces.

Plain-language explanation. A genre vector here has six dimensions, and a human decided what each one means. A real embedding model reads a film's plot, reviews, or dialogue and produces a vector with hundreds of dimensions — often 768 or more — where no single dimension has a clean human label. The model learned the layout of that space from data, not from a genre chart.

Why it matters. Every formula in this notebook — cosine similarity, dot product, Euclidean distance — applies unchanged to those 768-dimensional vectors. That's the whole trick: once "meaning" is numbers, the same three formulas power everything from this toy app to production-grade semantic search and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).

Pitfall Don't assume more dimensions automatically means "better." It means the model can represent finer shades of meaning — but it also means you can no longer point at one number and say "that's how much romance this has." The interpretability of this toy version is a genuine trade-off, not just a simplification for teaching.

What you now understand

Meaning becomes numbers

Any item — a film, a sentence, an image — can be represented as a vector, and that single move is what makes mathematical comparison possible.

Three lenses, one job

Cosine similarity, dot product, and Euclidean distance each answer "how similar?" slightly differently — direction only, direction plus magnitude, or raw distance.

Ranking is a scan and a sort

Compare a query vector against every candidate, then sort by score. That two-step shape scales from 39 films to a billion-row search index.
